A forest never recorded

Behind the cards of my game Spore & Skog there is a forest: songbirds, a cuckoo, a woodpecker, a bumblebee, crickets, frogs in the rain, an owl in the fog, ravens. None of them was recorded. Each call is made as it happens, sample by sample, by {{stat lines}} lines of plain Rust running inside a CPAL audio callback, and the game's Bevy systems tell it what weather it is.

This page runs that same Rust, compiled to WebAssembly. Step in.

by xaghoul · 17 October 2026 · Rust, CPAL, Bevy, DSP

The forest is quiet until you step in. Headphones help: the creatures are soft, and placed left and right.

The sliders are what the game sends when the weather over the forests changes. The bars are each creature's odds of calling next under that sky. In the game the creatures sit well under the music; alone here, they're brought up 12 dB.

Made as they call

Most game sounds are clips: a buffer made once (recorded, or rendered at start-up) and played back when it's needed. A forest doesn't work that way. A cuckoo that calls exactly the same three times in a row stops being a cuckoo and starts being a sound effect.

So the creatures in Spore & Skog aren't clips. Every call starts from a handful of dice (its pitch, how many notes, how fast, how far away, left or right) and is then made a sample at a time, on the audio thread, while you hear it. No two are the same, and there is nothing to store: the whole forest is {{stat kinds}} recipes and a little arithmetic per sample.

Live synthesis has a cost the clips don't: it runs against the audio deadline. So the forest is kept small. At most {{stat voices}} creatures call at once, each is a few oscillators and filters, and they sit well under everything else in the mix.

Each press makes a new call of that creature. Here it is rendered first, so the picture above is exactly what you hear; in the game the same code runs live, inside the callback.

One stream, one callback

CPAL is Rust's cross-platform audio output: CoreAudio, WASAPI, ALSA, Web Audio. It does one thing. It opens a stream and keeps calling your closure with an empty buffer to fill, a few milliseconds' worth at a time, on a real-time thread. Miss the deadline and you hear a click.

The game opens exactly one stream and puts a small mixer, the desk, in its callback. The rest of the game never touches the desk's state; it sends it commands over a channel, and the desk reads them at the start of each buffer:

Three small tools

Every creature is made from the same three things. First, noise: a xorshift generator, seeded, so the forest is the same every time the game starts, and with helpers for the dice.

{{item forest/src/wildlife.rs struct Noise + impl Noise}}

Second, a filter. Birds are mostly pure tones, but a woodpecker's knock, a bee's body, a frog's throat and a raven's croak are all something buzzing through a resonance. The tool for that is a state-variable filter, here in its "topology-preserving" form: stable when its frequency sweeps, cheap, and with a low-pass and a band-pass out of the same two lines of state.

{{item forest/src/wildlife.rs struct Svf + impl Svf}}

Third, a shape in time. Real calls don't switch on and off; they swell and fade. One smooth bump, a smoothstep up and down, shapes nearly every note in the forest:

{{item forest/src/wildlife.rs fn window}}

Each creature is then two pieces of one Call: the dice, rolled once in Call::new, and the sound, made a sample at a time in Call::next.

Whistles: songbird, cuckoo, owl

The three that sing share one engine: a list of notes, each a sweep from one pitch to another, played by a single sine. What makes them different is the notes they roll. A songbird throws a quick phrase of short sweeps around one high pitch:

{{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 1 Kind::Song}}

The cuckoo calls its falling third, two to four times, and always from a little way off:

{{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 1 Kind::Cuckoo}}

And the tawny owl, the fog's creature, calls its "hoooo …… hu, hu-hu-hoooo":

{{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 1 Kind::Owl}}

Then all three play through the same arm, each with its own timbre. The songbird is a bare sine in an arch. The cuckoo adds a touch of its octave. The owl breathes, with noise through the filter tuned to its octave, and its last note quavers as it starts:

{{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 2 Kind::Song}}

Knocks and buzzes: woodpecker, bumblebee, crickets

A woodpecker's roll is a train of knocks, each a burst of filtered noise with a ring of the dead wood's pitch, coming slightly faster as it goes and dying away:

{{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 1 Kind::Woodpecker}} {{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 2 Kind::Woodpecker}}

The bumblebee is the one creature that moves: it crosses from one side to the other, and its buzz (a sawtooth through the filter's low-pass) falls a little in pitch as it passes, the way a passing car's does.

Throats: frog and raven

These two are made the way voices are: a source and a filter. A frog's croak is a train of clicks (glottal pulses, 26 to 38 a second) each ringing a narrow resonance for the throat and a broader one an octave down for the chest. Nothing in it is a tone; the pitch you hear is the resonance.

{{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 2 Kind::Frog}}

The raven took the longest. Shaping a voice like the frog's, with pulses, jitter and the pitch slipping to half itself, kept sounding like a duck, or a kazoo. What finally worked is closer to a synthesizer patch: a low sawtooth (about 110 Hz) thrown up a fifth in 15 ms and falling back over 150 ms, chopped by a fast, nearly square tremolo, 55 times a second, the way a raven's throat flutters, its rate wavering so it never ticks like a machine. Then a strong "ah" at 1 kHz, a nasal edge just above it, and a lift at 2.5 to 4 kHz:

{{arm forest/src/wildlife.rs 2 Kind::Raven}}

Near and far

Every call, whatever made it, ends the same way. Each one rolled a distance (dull) when it began: far creatures are low-passed, because air and leaves take the highs first, and quieter. Then an equal-power pan puts it left or right, moving if it's the bee:

Who calls, and when

A forest in the rain shouldn't sound like a forest in the sun. Each creature has a habitat: how much it likes rain, fog, mild weather and a clear sky. Frogs love rain, the owl only comes out in fog, bees and crickets want it mild.

{{item forest/src/wildlife.rs impl Kind}}

To choose who calls next, the forest weighs each creature by how well it suits the weather about, with one more rule: whoever just called is four times less likely to call again, so you rarely hear the same creature twice running. (The bars at the top of the page are these weights.)

{{item forest/src/wildlife.rs fn choose}}

And when: every frame, a countdown. When it runs out, the next call comes, and the next wait is drawn between a third and nearly twice the mean, so calls neither queue up nor stall.

{{item forest/src/wildlife.rs fn next_frame}}

One honest note on the real-time rules: Call::new builds a small list of notes, an allocation on the audio thread. It happens a few times a minute, for a few bytes, and I've let it be. Everything per sample allocates nothing.

Into Bevy

Spore & Skog is a Bevy game, but its sound doesn't use bevy_audio. The audio lives entirely outside the ECS: one CPAL stream, one desk in its callback. Bevy's side is small, and it has three parts.

1. The stream is a non-send resource

A cpal::Stream plays for as long as it lives, and it isn't Send. So it's opened once, in an exclusive start-up system, and kept in the world as a non-send resource. Next to it goes a plain Sound resource holding the channel's sender: that is the only way the rest of the game reaches the audio.

{{item examples/bevy-forest/src/main.rs impl Plugin for SoundPlugin}} {{item examples/bevy-forest/src/main.rs fn open}}

The Sound resource is a thin set of methods that each send one command. If there's no audio device (or the game runs muted) the sender is None, and every method quietly does nothing, so no system ever has to ask.

{{item excerpts/sound.rs fn wildlife + fn send}}

2. One system decides what the forest sounds like

The interesting part is on the game's side: working out what the forest should be. In Spore & Skog three forests lie on the table, each with weather cards laid over it. This system runs every frame at the table. It takes the weather over all three, or over just one when you lean in to look at that forest's card, in which case the creatures come closer and call more often. It hushes them behind a menu and once the game is over. It sends a command only when something actually changed, remembered in a Local, so the channel carries a handful of messages a game, not sixty a second.

{{item excerpts/hooks.rs const TABLE_CALLS + const TABLE_GAIN + const LOOK_CALLS + const LOOK_GAIN + fn forest}}

Leaving the table, a system on OnExit sends one last command, and the forest falls quiet. The title screen has its own forest, busier and softer, following the weather that drifts across the logo:

{{item excerpts/title.rs const TITLE_CALLS + const TITLE_CALL_GAIN + fn hush}}

3. The app's lifecycle

On an iPad, the game can be sent to the home screen at any moment. The stream should stop then, and the synthesis with it. Bevy reports this as AppLifecycle messages:

{{item excerpts/sound.rs fn suspend}}

That's the whole integration. It's in this repository as a small runnable Bevy app with the game's own numbers, where the keyboard stands in for the table: cargo run -p bevy-forest --release. Keys 1, 2, 3 and 0 set the weather, Space leans in, M opens a "menu".

Testing a forest

You can't test that a cuckoo sounds like a cuckoo. You can test that the forest obeys the weather, that every creature is heard and then stops, and that calls come about as often as asked:

{{item forest/src/wildlife.rs fn the_weather_picks_the_creatures}} {{item forest/src/wildlife.rs fn calls_come_about_as_often_as_asked}}

To actually listen, one more test writes every creature, and forty seconds of each weather's forest, to WAV files: FOREST_WAV=/tmp/forest cargo test -p forest.

Hear it where it lives

The code on this page is open-sourced from Spore & Skog: The Divine Forage, a quick card duel of weather and secret mushroom spots.

Two forest gods share one sky over three Swedish forests. Lay rain, fog or mild weather over a forest, then harvest your hidden mushroom spots when the weather over them is right, before your Rival does. First to 35 mushrooms wins, and the forest you've been listening to is there the whole time, changing with every card.
